A Reading Comprehension (Passage 1)

Many cities are now turning unused spaces into “pocket parks.” These are small green areas built on empty lots, rooftops, or even beside busy roads. They are designed to give people a quiet place to rest in the middle of a noisy city.

One pocket park in Singapore sits on the roof of a shopping centre. It has grass, small trees, and several benches. Office workers often go there during their lunch break to eat, read, or simply sit in the sun. The park also collects rainwater, which is used to water the plants.

Another example is in New York, where a former car park was turned into a garden. Local people grow vegetables and flowers there, and children come to learn how plants grow. The garden is cared for by volunteers, so it costs the city very little money.

Pocket parks are not just pretty. Studies show that people who spend even ten minutes in a green space feel calmer and can think more clearly. For crowded cities, these small parks may be the simplest way to improve daily life.

1. What is a “pocket park” according to the passage?
2. Where is the pocket park in Singapore located?
3. Who takes care of the garden in New York?
4. What does the passage say about spending time in green spaces?
5. What is the main idea of the passage?

B Reading Comprehension (Passage 2)

Mia had always been afraid of the water. As a child, she refused to go near the sea, and even the sound of waves made her nervous. Her friends loved swimming, but Mia would sit on the beach and watch.

Everything changed during a school trip to the coast. The teacher announced that everyone would try kayaking (皮划艇). Mia felt sick with fear. But her best friend Lily sat beside her and said, “We’ll do it together. If you fall, I’ll be right there.”

The first ten minutes were terrible. Mia’s hands shook, and she kept looking down at the dark water. Then Lily started singing an old song they both knew. Slowly, Mia began to paddle in time with the song. She stopped looking down and started looking ahead — at the cliffs, the birds, the open sky.

By the end of the afternoon, Mia was laughing. She had not fallen once. More importantly, she had discovered that fear often grows in silence, and shrinks when someone stays beside you.

C Reading Comprehension (Passage 3)

For thousands of years, farmers have grown food. Today, however, much of our food travels long distances before it reaches our plates. This has led to a growing interest in “urban farming” — growing food in or near cities.

Urban farms can be found in many surprising places. Some use rooftops, where vegetables grow in boxes under the sun. Others use old factories, where plants grow under special lights without soil. These methods use much less water than traditional farms and can produce food all year round.

Supporters say urban farming has clear benefits. Fresh food reaches customers in hours, not days, so it tastes better and keeps more vitamins. It also creates jobs and brings neighbours together. In some cities, schools use small farms to teach children where food comes from.

However, urban farming also faces problems. Space is expensive, and artificial light uses a lot of electricity. Some experts warn that urban farms cannot replace traditional agriculture. Still, they agree that even small city farms can make our food system healthier and more connected to daily life.
